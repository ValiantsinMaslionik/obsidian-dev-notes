## Business Driver

Business Driver is an internal or external change or impact which indicates a reaction from the business. This impact is not necessarily negative.

**Examples:**
• Profitability (which is composed of two other drivers: Revenue and Costs)
• Increased tax in the country
• High number of leavers (attrition)
• Stockholders want to get high income in 3 years

A business driver is a resource, process or condition that is vital for the continued success and growth of a business. A company must
identify its business drivers and attempt to maximize any that are under their control. There are always outside business drivers that a
company cannot influence, such as economic conditions or trade relations with other nations.

## Business Goal

Business Goal represents a high-level statement of intent, direction, or desired end state for an organization and its stakeholders.

**Examples:**
• Build market in US
• Grow with 20% in every year during the next 3 years
• Decrease the operational cost by 10%
• Increase profit by $1 million this year

Goals are a fundamental part of any good business plan. They specify where your business is going and how you'll get there.

#### SMART vs FAST Goals

There are two approaches for correct goals identifying: SMART and FAST. The SMART goals methodology has been used by managers and business owners for decades. And in 2018, the MIT Sloan School of Management published a research article where FAST goals were introduced. Both these approaches are widely used nowadays, so let's more deeply explore each of them.

**SMART**
- **S**pecific
- **M**easurable
- **A**chievable
- **R**ealistic / relevant
- **T**ime-bound

This approach encourages you to ask the following questions when setting goals:
• What will be accomplished? What actions will you take?
• What data will measure the goal? How much? How well?
• Is the goal doable? Do you have the necessary skills and resources?
• How does the goal align with broader goals? Why is the result important?
• What is the time frame for accomplishing the goal?

Smart goals are designed in a way to foster a clear and mutual understanding of what constitutes expected levels of performance and successful professional development. Remember, goals are intended to focus attention and resources on what is most important so that you can be successful in achieving your priorities.

**FAST**
- **F**requently discussed
- **A**mbitious
- **S**pecific
- **T**ransparent

To drive strategy execution, leaders can set FAST goals, which are embedded in frequent discussions, ambitious in scope, measured by specific metrics and milestones, and transparent for everyone in the organization to see. FAST goals approach was pioneered by such companies as Google and Intel.

• **Frequently Discussed**. Many companies set goals on an annual basis. Management and employees typically enter these goals in a spreadsheet and then look at them once or twice over the course of the year. At year's end, sometimes accomplishments have little in common with what their goals had been at the year beginning. Making goals based on fiscal quarters rather than annually gives team members four times the opportunity to discuss those goals, evaluate them, and adjust them as needed. Monitoring progress and discussion are paramount to business success. Companies like Microsoft and IBM now discuss goals regularly rather than waiting for annual performance reviews.
• **Ambitious**. Ambitious goals are much more effective than conservative, achievable goals. If you're using SMART goals, you could set a goal to increase sales by 10 or 20 percent and be done with it. Setting an ambitious FAST goal, you may be inclined to try to double sales instead. After looking at ways to achieve that goal, discussing it, and then setting down specific actions to reach that goal and ways to measure it, you may find it isn't really realistic, but an increase of 50 percent could potentially be met. Google, for example, requires employees to achieve only between 60 to 70 percent of their targets. Compensation, including bonuses, isn't tied to these targets.
• **Specific**. Making goals specific, based on actions and measured with metrics and milestones, has been a part of Intel's business model since the 1970s. Other companies have since adopted this model, including Amazon, Intuit, and Google. *Goals should be defined with specific actions, measurable and clear in how they'll be achieved and measured. This is often defined as **objectives and key results (OKRs)***. Other companies measure goals using ***key performance indicators (KPIs)***. Defining just a few specific and ambitious goals can increase performance. A study conducted over the past five decades has shown this to increase performance to the 80th percentile.
• **Transparent**. Making goals transparent to everyone in your company may seem more detrimental than beneficial, especially if a company has always kept goals private and confidential in the past. However, in the vast majority of cases, the benefits outweigh any drawbacks, regardless of the company's size or the industry it's in. When the business makes its overall goals available to the employees, they, in turn, can align their
goals and objectives to match the company's strategies. When everyone knows what other departments are planning to do, they're less likely to make unrealistic demands of those departments and duplicate the work planned by those other departments.

## Business Objective

Business Objective is an actionable item needed to achieve business goals.

**Examples:**
- Business goal: increase profit by $1 million this year
	- Sales department objective: increase sales by 40% this year
	- Production department objective: decrease returns by 10%

Normally, both goals and objectives should be SMART/FAST.

Successful businesses are based on both goals and objectives, as they clarify the purpose of the business and help identify necessary
actions. Goals are general statements of desired achievement, while objectives are the specific steps or actions you take to reach your
goal. Both goals and objectives should be specific and measurable. Goals can involve areas such as profitability, growth and customer
service, with a range of objectives that can be used to meet those goals.

## Business Value

Business value is a generated value, such as concrete products or services.

**Examples**
- Soccer ball
- Car
- Consulting

Business value is the benefit(s) that a firm generates for its stakeholders. This includes a firm's long-term ability to create revenue,
products, services, employment, quality of life and investment returns. 

## Business Capability

A business capability is a  business function's abstraction, answering the question of what a company needs to be able to do to be successful.
Business Capability is a particular ability or capacity that a business may possess or exchange to achieve a specific purpose or outcome.

![[Pasted image 20250420173625.png]]

• Critically, a business capability delineates what a business does without attempting to explain how, why, or where
the business uses the capability.
• A combination of roles, processes, information, and tools enables a business capability.

**BUSINESS CAPABILITY CHARACTERISTICS**
- Unique
- Stable
- Abstracted from the organizational model
- Can be defined for any organizational unit
- Individual capabilities do not have a purpose

• **Each capability is unique.** A capability is a fundamental element of the organization and as such is clearly different from other capabilities. A capability might be applied throughout the organization and can be applied in different ways to affect different outcomes, but it is still a single сapability.
• **Capabilities are stable.** Well-defined capabilities rarely change. They provide a much more stable view of organizations than do projects, processes, applications, or even strategies. Capabilities only change when there is a significant shift in the underlying business model or mission which might occur through a business transformation initiative or in conjunction with a new merger or acquisition. This stability is a major part of their appeal.
• **Capabilities are abstracted from the organizational model.** Capability models are not just a simple restatement of the enterprise’s organizational model. They are organizationally neutral which means that changes in the organizational structure don’t require a change in the capability model. In simple organizations, the capability model may indeed look similar to the corporate organizational structure; however, in more-complex organizations capabilities both common to and unique to organizational units are found.
• **Capabilities can be defined for any organizational unit**. Though most architects think of capabilities at the enterprise level, every organization, large or small, has a set of capabilities it applies to fulfill its mission. Since capabilities represent the organization’s leader’s interest, each organization will have a unique set of capabilities not seen in the rest of the organization. Therefore, each organization will have a unique capability map.
• **Individual capabilities do not have a purpose**. Since capabilities represent what an organization can do and not what it actually does they can be viewed as potential. This means capabilities can be applied in multiple ways, for different purposes. This also means they must be assessed in terms of their application - measured on a fit-for-purpose scale not a maturity scale.
